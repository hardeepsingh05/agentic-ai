**Motion: There should be strict laws to regulate LLMs – In Favor**

1. **The stakes are too high to leave AI unchecked**  
   Large Language Models (LLMs) now generate text, code, images, and audio that can be indistinguishable from human‑created content. Their reach is unprecedented: from news feeds and educational materials to legal advice, medical diagnoses, and financial trading. When such powerful tools are deployed without oversight, they can spread misinformation, perpetuate bias, manipulate public opinion, and even endanger lives. History shows that transformative technologies—whether the printing press, radio, or the internet—required legal frameworks to protect societies from their worst abuses. LLMs are no exception.

2. **Current self‑regulation is insufficient and often self‑defeating**  
   Tech companies promise “responsible AI” but prioritize profit and rapid deployment over safety. Internal audits are opaque, conflict‑of‑interest‑free, and rarely enforced. In practice, we have seen LLMs hallucinate facts, reproduce hate speech, and generate deep‑fake scams at scale. Companies can—and do—modify models after release without notifying users, making it impossible for regulators, researchers, or affected individuals to hold them accountable. Voluntary guidelines cannot close these gaps.

3. **Strict laws provide a level playing field and protect competition**  
   Without uniform legal standards, smaller developers and open‑source projects are forced to compete with well‑funded conglomerates that can absorb the cost of compliance violations. Clear regulations create predictable compliance costs, encouraging innovation while ensuring that all players meet baseline safety, transparency, and accountability requirements. This prevents monopolistic control over AI capabilities that could otherwise dictate the future of information ecosystems.

4. **Regulation safeguards fundamental rights and democratic processes**  
   • **Freedom of expression:** Laws can define the line between protected speech and coordinated disinformation campaigns, protecting both creators and consumers.  
   • **Privacy:** LLMs trained on personal data must adhere to strict data‑use protocols, respecting GDPR‑style rights.  
   • **Non‑discrimination:** Mandatory bias audits and impact assessments will force developers to identify and mitigate discriminatory outputs, protecting marginalized groups.  
   • **Due process:** Legal frameworks can mandate transparent model cards, provenance logs, and avenues for redress when AI‑generated content causes harm.

5. **International coordination prevents regulatory arbitrage**  
   LLMs operate globally; a model trained in one jurisdiction can instantly influence discourse worldwide. Strict, harmonized laws—perhaps modeled on the EU’s AI Act, complemented by standards from the OECD and UN—ensure that harmful content, deep‑fakes, and malicious code cannot simply be outsourced to jurisdictions with lax oversight. This collective approach maintains global trust in AI technologies.

6. **The economic case for regulation**  
   Studies estimate that unchecked AI misinformation could cost the global economy trillions in lost productivity, market instability, and legal liabilities. Proactive regulation reduces the probability of catastrophic failures, saving far more than the compliance costs. Moreover, consumer confidence in AI‑driven services rises when robust safeguards are in place, driving adoption and growth in legitimate AI markets.

7. **Precedent and feasibility**  
   Similar regulatory regimes exist for pharmaceuticals, financial services, and broadcasting. They combine pre‑market certification, ongoing monitoring, and penalties for non‑compliance. Applying comparable mechanisms to LLMs—such as certification of high‑risk models, mandatory risk‑management plans, and real‑time takedown procedures—is technically feasible and legally sound.

**Conclusion**  
The power of LLMs to reshape society is a double‑edged sword. Without strict, enforceable laws, we risk irreversible damage to truth, democracy, and public safety. Well‑crafted regulation does not stifle innovation; it channels it responsibly, protects rights, ensures fair competition, and preserves trust. The motion is therefore not merely advisable—it is imperative.
